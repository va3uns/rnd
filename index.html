India’s Moving Protest Site

How far can dissent walk before it has left the city?

Spatial criticism · 2026 · continues my bachelor’s thesis from 2021

Delhi rarely bans protest outright. It moves it. A street is designated, a lawn is fenced, a border is sealed, a permit is withdrawn, and each time the public lands a little further from the Parliament it came to speak to. This piece follows that movement, place by place, and asks what it does to a democracy.

A century of moving sites

My bachelor’s thesis (2021) traced a century of Delhi’s protest sites: from the old city before 1947, to Ramlila Maidan, to the Boat Club lawns across from Parliament. After farmers occupied those lawns in 1988, gatherings there were banned, and by 1993 protest had been pushed onto one street, Jantar Mantar. Comparing the two sites with space syntax showed why: the Boat Club’s large blocks and few entrances make a crowd easy to hold, while Jantar Mantar’s small blocks and alleys let it spill and scatter. The thesis ended at the city’s edge, where farmers were camped in early 2021.

After the border, 2021–2026

  • Nov 2021 The farm laws are repealed after a year of camps at Delhi’s borders.
  • Sep 2022 Rajpath, beside the old Boat Club lawns, reopens as Kartavya Path.
  • May 2023 The wrestlers’ sit-in at Jantar Mantar is cleared on the day the new Parliament opens. Police say future sit-ins may only use a “notified place” other than Jantar Mantar.
  • Feb 2024 Marching farmers are stopped at the Punjab–Haryana border, about 200 km out, with concrete, containers, barbed wire and tear gas.
  • Jul 2024 Section 144, the colonial-era ban on assembly, becomes Section 163 of a new criminal code, with the same powers.
  • Jun 2026 A Gen Z movement that began as an online joke, the Cockroach Janta Party, fills Jantar Mantar over exam paper leaks.
  • Jul 2026 Its march from Jantar Mantar towards Parliament is stopped at barricades.
  • Oct 2026 Permission for Jantar Mantar is refused, entry is restricted at 45 metro stations and thousands are detained. The protest does not end. It spreads across markets and squares.

Reading the fence

I am documenting these places and reading them critically: the designated street, the fenced lawn, the sealed border, the closed metro station, the permit itself. What does it mean when a democracy decides where dissent may stand, and what does each new boundary say about who the city is for?

Other cities, other fences

The same question is being fought over in other places right now. Marches for Gaza that governments try to ban or reroute. Serbian students blockading faculties and walking from city to city. Roundabouts and stations blocked across France. No Kings rallies spread over thousands of small towns instead of one capital square. Each one tests where a public is still allowed to appear, and each one finds a different way around the fence.

The detailed report will set Delhi’s moving site beside these, through maps, images and the walking camp above: a counter-world in which the sit-in carries its own ground with it.

Spatialising Protests

Perceiving the role of urban public spaces in the culture of dissent in New Delhi, India

Bachelor’s thesis, Navrachana University · 2021 · supervisor: Prof. Pratyush Shankar

Themes

Urban public space · Social encounters · Collective behaviour · Qualitative research · Space syntax

Question

How can a city support or inhibit protest through its spatial characteristics? And how did Jantar Mantar become the “protest square” of India’s capital?

Method

I traced how Delhi’s protest sites moved from the old city to Lutyens’ Delhi, using historical maps and secondary literature. I then compared two sites, Jantar Mantar and the Boat Club Lawns, with space syntax analysis (visibility graph and agent-based analysis), site photographs from January 2021 and informal conversations. The work is qualitative.

What I found

Governments have repeatedly moved the official protest site farther from the political centre: first from the Boat Club to Jantar Mantar, after a mass farmers’ occupation in 1988.

The two sites shape crowds differently. Jantar Mantar sits among small blocks and alleys, so a crowd spills into the streets and is hard to contain. The Boat Club has large blocks and few entrances, so the crowd is bounded and easier to control. Symbolic value draws people in, but the surrounding street layout shapes how the crowd behaves and how the state responds.

I also drew four ways people occupy space when they protest: occupy, monument, agora and shelter.

What I would do next

The thesis calls its findings a preliminary step. The next step is to watch how people actually use these spaces, not only how the maps describe them.

I have started to carry it forward: India’s Moving Protest Site.

Investigating the challenges of Agile Project Management at RWE

Master’s thesis, Hochschule Rhein-Waal · 2025 · with Ayush Bhamu · supervisors: Prof. Dr. Kai Essig, Mr. Jan Ely

Themes

Human-computer interaction · Qualitative research · Interviews · Thematic analysis · Collaboration and organisational culture

Question

What challenges come up when a large German energy company adopts Scrum, and which structural and cultural factors push teams back to plan-driven ways of working?

Method

An embedded single-case study at RWE with nine semi-structured interviews: developers, solution managers, a product owner, UX/UI designers and a team lead. We coded the transcripts together using reflexive thematic analysis, and grouped the results at management, team and individual level. We treated recordings as personal data under GDPR and masked names, projects and dates.

What we found

Many teams ran Scrum events but kept top-down decision-making: Agile on paper. Scope changed mid-Sprint, approvals arrived after work was marked done, and roles and coaching were unclear. Teams doing feature work and interrupt-driven work in the same Sprint often fit Kanban better than Scrum.

Agile lasts when governance, funding and compliance move at the same pace as iteration, and when leaders back team autonomy and learning.

Limits

One organisation, nine interviews, and no delivery metrics or direct observation of ceremonies, so the findings should be transferred with care.

Stri Mandala

Design competition “rip. mix. burn.” · 2022 · with Pranjali Ambekar · published on FOLIO

Themes

Speculative design · Gender-inclusive public space · Right to the city · Urban futures · Visual critique

Reading the title

स्त्री (strī) is the Sanskrit and Hindi word for “woman”. मण्डल (maṇḍala) means “circle”. A mandala is a geometric, circular or square design that stands for the universe, wholeness and spiritual balance in Hinduism, Buddhism and Jainism. So the title reads “circle of women”: women as the centre that holds a city together.

Brief

The competition “rip. mix. burn.” asked entrants to use drawing to critique the existing city and reimagine the future city. We chose to ask what kind of space supports a woman’s growth as an individual, within a community and in a progressive society, and to argue for gender-inclusive public space.

What the drawing shows

The map of Jaipur is drawn as a rug. Women sit around it, weaving it. Jaipur is known for its hand-made rugs, so the drawing also shows the fine detail and patterns of that craft. The idea is that women have woven many Indian cities through their work, and that this contribution is rarely recognised. The map is the fabric, and the weavers are the ones who made it.

Why a drawing

The drawing does not predict a future. It asks who a city is made by and for, and it makes the answer visible: the right to the city is shaped by gender, and the people shaping it are often not seen. I see this as a first step toward speculative design: a deliberately imagined scene that makes a social question easy to discuss.

Wetterbank

Design fiction and research project · 2026 – ongoing · self-initiated · va3uns.github.io/wetterbank

Wetterbank is a bench that starts conversations between strangers, by helping them file complaints about the weather. It stands at a Düsseldorf tram stop and speaks like a public office that takes the weather very seriously. The bench does not exist. Yet.

  1. The weather is the safest way to start a conversation.
  2. +The tram is late. Again.
  3. +Nobody at the stop says a word.
  4. =A bench that speaks first.

Themes

Design fiction · Talking street furniture · Social encounters · Small talk · Humour as critique

Question

How do interactive and autonomous systems shape social encounters in public space?

I ask it in three parts. Do strangers at a tram stop want to talk at all? When a bench speaks first, does it open a conversation between people, or quietly replace it? And how will we respond once talking objects are ordinary, and should they be?

The real starting point

Germans talk about the weather a lot: 62% do so several times a week or more (YouGov, 3,290 adults, April 2026), and it is the most accepted small-talk topic, at 70% (Babbel/YouGov, 2025). Yet 48.5% of 18–24-year-olds find small talk uncomfortable, and 18–29-year-olds carry the highest loneliness burden (14.1% in 2021, Einsamkeitsbarometer 2024).

My reading: the opener is easy, the step after it is hard. Wetterbank is built for that step.

Sources: YouGov, t-online / Babbel, Einsamkeitsbarometer 2024

Three modes, one step at a time

Each mode moves a little further from the safe topic and a little closer to the person next to you. The bench only moves on when people respond, and its last step is to go quiet.

Two results that look the same

From a distance, A and B look the same: someone at the bench is talking. Only B is what Wetterbank is for. A bench you keep chatting with is just a smart speaker outdoors.

Try it

A whole bench cannot be prototyped on a website, so a complaint form stands in for it. You speak first, and the bench answers in its own voice. There is no AI behind it, only replies I wrote by hand, and nothing you type is sent or stored.

File a weather complaint

Planned studies

  1. Talking to people at tram stops. Short interviews, no bench.
  2. Interviewing the bench. A speculative conversation with the bench, published as a transcript.
  3. Becoming the bench. A performer plays the bench with limited senses and a fixed script.
  4. The staged tram stop. Actors play the bench and the strangers, and the audience discusses it afterwards.
  5. The ordinary bench. A visit to a chat bench with a sign. It is the baseline for everything else.
  6. A real bench, a hidden voice. A person nearby chooses the bench’s lines at a real stop. Nothing is recorded, and everyone is told afterwards.

They run from cheap to real. What I want to know first is small: does anyone get past the weather, and if so, was it their idea or the bench’s?

Limits

This is a design fiction, not a study. None of the six studies has been run, and the numbers describe weather talk in general, not benches.

KaLink

Applied research project, Hochschule Rhein-Waal · 2024 · with Luna Popovic and Samson Akande · supervisors: Prof. Dr. Kai Essig, Prof. Frank Feyrer · presented to the City of Kamp-Lintfort

Themes

Social connection · Urban community · Language and cultural barriers · Human-centred design · Qualitative research

Question

How do international students and long-time residents of Kamp-Lintfort interact, and what keeps the two groups apart?

Method

We ran eight semi-structured interviews with students, university staff and residents aged 20 to 48, and analysed them with inductive thematic coding. From this we built four personas and ran four open card-sorting sessions. We then tested a clickable prototype with six participants, and a revised version with ten, using think-aloud sessions.

What we found

Students and residents lived in separate channels: separate WhatsApp and Facebook groups, so information about events rarely crossed from one group to the other. The language barrier sat underneath this. One student told us: “The other international students are in the same situation so we gather together, and I feel the locals have their whole life set here and I am an intruder.”

Both groups wanted shared events and ways to exchange goods and help. Out of this we designed an app with three parts: a community space, events and a marketplace. Participants in both rounds of testing received it positively, and we presented the project to the City of Kamp-Lintfort.

Limits

We did not include older residents in the card sorting, the prototype is not fully functional, and we did not test whether the app would bring people together in practice.

What it opened up

The interviews were about more than an app. They were about how people from separate groups come to share a place. That is the question I would like to pursue beyond the screen.
